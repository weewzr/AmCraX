## 1. AmCraX: title and value proposition
*Start 0:00, about 29 s*

AmCraX is our ammonia cracking simulation and optimisation platform. Aspen Plus V15 is the engine, an independent Python model checks it, and a dashboard and a public website show the results. At 600 degrees and 20 bar, Aspen converts 98.1 percent of the ammonia into about 251.6 kilograms of hydrogen per hour, and 279 Aspen runs stand behind the deck. The honest limit: this is a steady-state design study, not a plant design.

## 2. Moving hydrogen is harder than making it
*Start 0:29, about 26 s*

Hydrogen is easy to make and hard to move. Compressing it from one to seven hundred bar costs about 6.0 kilowatt-hours per kilogram, and one life-cycle study assumes 5 percent is lost on the way. A refuelling station needs around 400 kilograms a day at 350 bar, and fuel cells accept only 0.1 parts per million ammonia. These are literature figures, not our results.

## 3. Ammonia carries hydrogen as an easy-to-store liquid
*Start 0:54, about 27 s*

Why ammonia? By mass it is 17.8 percent hydrogen, and the only other atom is nitrogen, so there is no carbon to manage. It stays liquid at about 9.9 bar at room temperature, so one cubic metre carries roughly 107 kilograms of hydrogen. Those density and pressure values are from the literature. The catch is that ammonia is toxic and the hydrogen still has to be released.

## 4. Cracking releases the hydrogen, but it costs heat
*Start 1:21, about 25 s*

To use the hydrogen we crack ammonia back into nitrogen and hydrogen. The reaction absorbs about 45.92 kilojoules per mole, so releasing hydrogen takes around 30.6 kilojoules per mole of hydrogen, versus under one for evaporating liquid hydrogen. Industry already offers large crackers, but the technology readiness is quoted at only 4 to 6. Heat is therefore the design problem AmCraX targets.

## 5. Rigorous simulation, independent checks, open results
*Start 1:46, about 29 s*

AmCraX has four parts. Aspen Plus V15 solves the flowsheet, with the input files generated and run from Python. An independent set of 9 Python packages recomputes the physics so we check Aspen rather than trust it. A FastAPI backend and a dashboard with 11 pages let you explore every case, and a public website shows the published results. 391 Python tests pass. It is a design-study tool, not a control system.

## 6. One flowsheet: liquid ammonia in, purified hydrogen out
*Start 2:15, about 33 s*

Here is the whole process. Liquid ammonia at 100 kilomoles per hour is pumped, vaporised and preheated by the hot product gas in a feed-effluent exchanger, then cracked at 600 degrees and 20 bar. The cooled gas goes to a PSA unit that recovers 0.85 of the hydrogen, and the tail gas is burned to supply heat. The PSA is a reduced-order split, not a designed adsorber, and its 0.85 recovery is our assumption, above the 0.71 to 0.81 reported in the literature.

## 7. Aspen Plus V15 solves the full flowsheet in seconds
*Start 2:48, about 27 s*

This is a genuine Aspen Plus V15 screenshot of our model, not a mock-up. The cracker is an RGibbs equilibrium reactor with the Peng-Robinson method, and the baseline converges without errors in about 2.0 seconds. Behind the deck are 279 Aspen runs: 20 flowsheet cases, 91 sensitivity runs and a 168-point equilibrium grid, all converged. An equilibrium reactor is optimistic: a real catalyst bed may not reach it.

## 8. Two independent models agree on equilibrium; kinetics size the bed
*Start 3:15, about 31 s*

To trust Aspen we rebuilt the equilibrium independently in Python and compared like with like. With Peng-Robinson in both, the largest gap over 84 points is 0.19 percentage points; with ideal gas in both it is 0.05 percentage points. A kinetic plug-flow model then sizes the bed: about 113 kilograms of nickel catalyst against 25 of ruthenium to reach 99 percent of equilibrium. The limit: those kinetics were fitted at one atmosphere and are extrapolated to 20 bar.

## 11. At 600 °C and 20 bar, Aspen converts 98.1% of the ammonia
*Start 4:41, about 30 s*

These are the baseline results from Aspen. At 600 degrees and 20 bar, 98.1 percent of the ammonia converts, giving 251.6 kilograms of hydrogen an hour with 2,293 kilowatts of external heat. The first-law efficiency is 87.7 percent, an upper bound. The map shows why we stop at 600 degrees: at 20 bar, conversion rises only 1.4 points more by 800 degrees. ## 12. The feed/effluent exchanger cuts external heat by 21% in Aspen
*Start 5:11, about 27 s*

Heat is where the design is won. In Aspen, the feed/effluent exchanger recovers 612 kilowatts and cuts external heating by 21 percent, from 2,905 kilowatts to 2,293 kilowatts, lifting first-law efficiency from 82.4 percent to 87.7 percent. Our Python pinch analysis on the Aspen streams sets targets: burning the tail gas could bring imports down to about 753 kilowatts. Those are targets, not a designed exchanger network.

## 15. Every number carries its source and its check
*Start 6:34, about 28 s*

Who would use this? Teams screening crackers at import terminals and hubs, engineers testing what heat integration is worth, and teachers. What is different is traceability: every value names its source, and Aspen and an independent model check each other. Of 137 validation checks, 105 pass and 22 fail, and we publish the failures: mostly two models using different feed-section property methods. We do not claim a validated digital twin.
